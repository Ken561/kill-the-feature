# Lessons Learned

**Classification: Portfolio scenario.**

## What This Exercise Reinforced

**A feature can be easy to build and still be the wrong thing to build.** The technical feasibility of AI Meeting Summary was never in question — the value, risk, and opportunity-cost analysis is where the real decision lived.

**Broad framing inflates apparent value.** "Every meeting, every employee" made the opportunity look larger than the evidence supported. Sizing against the actual segment with an unserved need — not the total possible population — produced a materially different, more honest picture.

**The most valuable output of a kill decision is the redirect, not just the "no."** Stopping at "don't build this" leaves the organization's appetite for an AI showcase feature unaddressed. Naming a specific, better-evidenced alternative (enterprise knowledge retrieval) turned the decision into forward progress rather than just a veto.

**Privacy risk compounds when you change what's ephemeral into what's persistent.** The sharpest risk wasn't about AI accuracy — it was about turning previously unrecorded, spontaneous conversation into a stored, searchable, potentially discoverable record. That's a different category of risk than most feature evaluations account for by default.

## What Would Make This Analysis Stronger

- Real usage data on existing transcription-tool adoption, rather than an assumed estimate, would sharpen the opportunity sizing considerably.
- Direct input from Legal and HR on the retention and discoverability risk, rather than this analysis's own reasoning about it, before treating that risk assessment as final.

## Revisit Trigger

If a specific team demonstrates a validated, high-frequency need for meeting summarization that existing tools genuinely don't meet — and proposes a scope limited to meeting types employees have explicitly opted into — this decision should be revisited with that narrower, evidenced case in hand, not reopened on the strength of the original broad pitch.
